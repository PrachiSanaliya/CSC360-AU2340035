## Linear Equations and Matrices

- Linear equations can be represented using matrices and vectors.
- The general form discussed was:

  **A × x = b**

  where:
  - **A** is a matrix of coefficients.
  - **x** is the vector containing the unknown variables.
  - **b** is the resultant vector.

- Example:

  **2x + 3y = 5**

- A vector containing two variables can be represented as:

  **x = (x, y)**

- The solutions of a system of linear equations can be different depending on the equations.
- Types of solutions discussed included:
  - Unique solution
  - Infinite solutions
  - Other cases where a consistent solution may not exist.

## Row and Column Vectors

- Vectors can be represented in different forms.
- A **row vector** is written horizontally.
- A **column vector** is written vertically.
- Matrix multiplication follows specific dimension rules.
- Multiplying an **n × n matrix** with an **n × 1 column vector** produces an **n × 1 resultant vector**.

## Rank of a Matrix

- The concept of **rank of a matrix** was discussed.
- Rank represents the number of linearly independent rows or columns in a matrix.
- A matrix can have **full rank** when its rank reaches the maximum possible value for its dimensions.
- A **singular matrix** does not have full rank and has dependent rows or columns.

## Dependency of Vectors

- The dependency of vectors in a matrix can be checked.
- Vectors are **linearly dependent** when one vector can be represented using a combination of the other vectors.
- Linear independence is related to the rank of the matrix.
- Checking vector dependency helps determine the nature of the system of linear equations.

## Stack and Queue

Two basic data structures were discussed:

### Stack

- A stack follows **LIFO (Last In, First Out)**.
- The element inserted last is removed first.
- Basic operations include:
  - `push`
  - `pop`

### Queue

- A queue follows **FIFO (First In, First Out)**.
- The element inserted first is removed first.
- Basic operations include:
  - `enqueue`
  - `dequeue`

## Checking a Point Inside a Circle

- A mathematical condition can be used to determine whether a point lies inside a circle.
- The distance between the point and the centre of the circle is compared with the radius.
- If the distance is less than or equal to the radius, the point lies inside or on the circle.

## Distance Formula

- The distance between two points was discussed.
- For two points:

  **P₁(x₁, y₁)** and **P₂(x₂, y₂)**

  the distance is:

  **d = √((x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²)**

- The formula is based on the relationship between the horizontal and vertical differences between the two points.

## Proof of the Distance Formula

- The distance formula can be derived using the **Pythagorean theorem**.
- The difference in the x-coordinates gives one side of the right triangle.
- The difference in the y-coordinates gives the other side.
- The distance between the two points represents the hypotenuse.
- Therefore:

  **d² = (x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²**

  and hence:

  **d = √((x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²)**

- This distance calculation can also be applied to graphical problems, such as checking whether a point is within a circle.
